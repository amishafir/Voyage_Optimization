What is a square, exactly?

A two-line description of the wave and current sources opened a question about spatial resolution. Checking it against the API rather than the paper dissolves the premise: all three fields arrive at the same resolution. What remains is a sampling design that cannot resolve the grid its own data comes on, and a model that discards most of what it collects.

Scope: design and sampling mechanism only No measurement of collected weather values

Where it started

Two adjacent bullets in §5.1.1, describing the forecast sources.

paper_full_draft.tex — lines 773–777
  • Waves (significant wave height) — the Météo-France wave model (MFWAM), a third-generation spectral ocean-wave model, at 0.25° resolution, re-initialised twice daily, to 168 h.
  • Ocean currents (velocity and direction) — the Météo-France SMOC ocean model, at 0.08° resolution, re-initialised once daily, to 168 h.

Two fields from the same provider, one line apart, at resolutions differing by a factor of three, while the model declares sea conditions constant within a 0.5° × 0.5° cell over a six-hour block. If each component arrives on its own grid, what is the square we hold constant, and is composing three granularities into one unit legitimate?

The premise does not survive contact with the API

Open-Meteo returns the resolved grid cell for every request. Sweeping coordinates and reading those back recovers each endpoint's grid exactly — no inference required.

All three fields arrive at essentially the same resolution — about 0.08°, or 3–5 NM. Not 0.25° for wind against 0.08° for the marine fields. The premise that opened this investigation, that the components come at heterogeneous granularities needing reconciliation, is not the case.

One real asymmetry survives, and it concerns alignment, not scale. The marine grid is regular and edge-registered, so it tiles cleanly against any box drawn on multiples of 1/12°. The wind grid aligns with nothing — Gaussian latitude rows with a point count that changes row to row cannot be made to tile against the marine grid, against a regular box, or against itself. No single square can be drawn that respects both sources.

Two further facts follow from the request as written, both from the documentation. models is never set, so best_match selects the highest-resolution applicable model — hence ECMWF rather than GFS. And cell_selection is never set, so the Marine API defaults to sea, which is correct for us, while the Forecast API defaults to land, which finds a grid cell on land of similar elevation. On an ocean route that is the wrong default, and it is a plausible explanation for the known Port B behaviour.

What our sampling can and cannot resolve

The question is not what the weather does; it is what our sampling design is capable of seeing. That is fixed by the source grid and the waypoint spacing alone.

Two scales must not be conflated. There is the scale at which the weather is genuinely constant — a property of the data, established from the API's grid definition — and the scale at which our sampling mechanism is entitled to assert it. We can never claim constancy below our own sampling interval, because we hold no observation there. Resolving a feature of size L requires sampling at L/2 or finer.

The epistemic position, stated exactly

Neither route's sampling can resolve the grid its own data arrives on. Route 1 is five times coarser than the source cell and could not resolve anything below 50 NM. Route 2 sits at roughly the cell scale — it lands about one sample per cell and can never see inside one.

So the true constancy scale is 3–5 NM, and we know that from the API's grid, not from anything we collected. Our own dataset cannot confirm it and, on route 1, contains no information about spatial structure finer than 25 NM at all.

The square our sampling can actually support

It follows that there is exactly one square we are entitled to draw: the interval between consecutive waypoints. Inside it we hold precisely one reading, so the forecast is constant across it by construction — not because the atmosphere is uniform there, but because that is the resolution of our knowledge. Any finer square subdivides a single number; any coarser square merges readings that are genuinely independent.

There is a hard limit here that has to be stated plainly, because it is easy to talk oneself out of. No practical segment stays inside one source tile. A track running diagonally across a 1/12° grid crosses latitude and longitude boundaries alike, so even route 2's 5 NM segment passes through a median of three tiles, and route 1's 25 NM segment through eight. Measured over both routes, the share of segments confined to a single tile is 0.0%.

Getting a segment inside one tile would need a length of roughly 1 NM or less — below the 1.58 NM median distance between a sample and the position its value belongs to. So it is unachievable in principle, not merely unaffordable: we cannot localise a reading to a tile more precisely than the tile is wide. The graph square is therefore never a region of constant forecast, and cannot be made one. It is a region over which we hold one reading, drawn from one of the several tiles it crosses. Route 2 is 2.7× better than route 1 on that count — three tiles rather than eight — which is a real difference in quality but not a difference in kind.

Where inside the square does the value actually live?

Every value sits at the centre of its tile — but that centre is the model's grid point, not our waypoint. We ask at an arbitrary position inside the tile and receive the number computed at its centre, so each sample carries a small, bounded position error.

This is the one caveat that belongs beside the waypoint-interval square. On route 1 the offset is 8% of the square and can be ignored. On route 2 it is 32% — the sample's true position can sit a third of a square from where the graph places it. That is not an argument against the square; it is the reason not to go finer than 5 NM there, since below about 5 NM the position uncertainty of each sample would exceed the square it is being placed in.

Worth noting for the record: the API returns the resolved centre with every response, and the collector discards it — collector.py reads only the values. The offset is therefore unrecoverable from the stored data, though it would be a one-line change to keep it.

The square is not a geographic cell; it is one sampling interval. Within it the forecast is constant because we hold a single reading, and that is the strongest claim the sampling permits. Stated that way it is exact and defensible on both routes — and it is not the claim the paper currently makes.

We collect hourly and then discard five hours in six. Every forecast issue stores hourly lead times out to the 7-day default, so a one-hour block requires no new collection, no API budget, and no re-run of the campaign. The capability is already in the file.

§5.1.1 justifies the 6 h block from the GFS refresh cycle. That cycle justifies the re-planning cadence — how often a new forecast becomes available is exactly the right reason to re-plan every 6 h. It says nothing about how long weather may be held constant. The forecast issue is 6-hourly; the forecast itself is hourly. The paper conflates two different quantities under one number.

Nine steps take an SWS and the cell's weather to an SOG. Four touch no weather at all.

The fuel rate consumes no weather whatsoever. FCR = 0.000706 · Vs³  (mt h⁻¹, floor 0.1) It is a pure cubic in still-water speed. Weather has exactly one point of entry into the objective: the solver fixes an arc's SOG from its distance and duration, inverts Vs = g−1(Vg; w) by binary search through steps 1–9, and prices the resulting SWS cubically.

Of the four values reaching that inversion, one is an integer 0–12, two describe the current, and one is a bearing. That is the entire weather content of the cost function — and §5.1.1 spends three bullets documenting the resolution, cadence and horizon of products supplying rather less than that.

What is actually wrong

Ranked by consequence for the paper's claims, not by size of the code change.

The paper's governing SOG equation does not describe the implemented function

Critical

Equation (eq:sog) states Vg = Vs − ΔVwind − ΔVwave + Vc,∥ with Vc,∥ = Vccos θc. Both halves are wrong.

There are no separate wind and wave terms — the code applies one combined Beaufort-driven loss. The current is not an along-track projection but a full 2-D vector synthesis, which is why a pure beam current raises SOG (12.50 → 12.86 kn at 3 kn abeam) rather than leaving along-track progress unchanged.

physics.py:292-316 · :234-236

Significant wave height is not an input — and the paper says both things

Critical

Waves affect the model only through the Beaufort number, whose scale definition already encodes a characteristic sea state. The paper states this correctly at line 247 — "wave condition measured in Beaufort units" — then contradicts it at line 358: "the wave added-resistance loss scales with significant wave height Hw and the beam-to-length ratio." Neither quantity appears in the speed path.

physics.py:249 declared · :446 passed · never an operand · collector.py:137 BN from wind speed

Segment-boundary nodes are priced with the previous segment's heading

Critical

position_at_d resolves a distance lying exactly on a segment boundary to the segment that ends there, so every path gets the outgoing leg's first stretch priced with the incoming heading. Route 1's first turn is 61.25° → 121.53°, so that stretch is priced 60° off, which feeds straight into the relative wind angle and the resistance. This is independent of how the squares are drawn and survives any re-placement of the lines.

geo_grid.py:246 · route 1 — 11/11 interior boundaries · route 2 — 9/9

The 0.5° cell corresponds to nothing, and five defects follow from it

Critical

It is neither the source grid (~0.08°) nor our sampling interval (25 / 5 NM). Because it matches neither, five separate faults arise, all of which disappear when the squares are drawn at the sampling interval instead:

  1. Cell identity is a coin-flip on the boundary. A node sitting on a 0.5° line has floor(lat/0.5) landing on an integer, so arcs departing it are priced with the cell being left about half the time — 80/152 crossings on route 1, 56/111 on route 2. A guard existed in build_edges.lookup_source_state and was lost in the refactor.
  2. Currents are averaged as magnitude and bearing separately, not as vectors, so a cell spanning a reversal manufactures current instead of cancelling it: 1 kn@090° with 1 kn@270° returns 1.000 kn@180° where the true mean is zero.
  3. Beaufort is quantised before averaging — round(mean(BN)) rather than BN(mean(Vwind)) — an undocumented choice with no small consequences, since one BN step doubles Cform at low Beaufort.
  4. Route 1's cells are frequently empty and fetch a neighbour's reading, caching it as their own; route 2's average four independent readings into one. One declared assumption, two different operators, on the paper's two experiments.
  5. Cell size varies with latitude — 29.3 NM of longitude on route 1 against 18.3 NM on route 2 — so route 2 has a denser decision grid by geometry alone. The paper attributes its larger SR–Luo gap entirely to weather severity.
weather.py:386-461 · geo_grid.py:216-219 · resolved by the redesign below

The spatial grid is a function of the treatment variable

High

H-lines are filtered for τ-feasibility against the speed band, and the band is derived from mean_sog ± 3 = f(distance, ETA). Changing the ETA changes which cells exist in the graph. For a study whose independent variable is the ETA, that is a design problem in itself.

nodes.py:214-225 ← SR_main.py:159-161

Beaufort is computed before averaging, not after

Medium

BN is derived per waypoint at collection, so the pipeline evaluates round(mean(BNi)) rather than BN(mean(Vwind,i)). Averaging the physical quantity and then quantising is arguably the more defensible order, and a ±1 disagreement is never small — at low Beaufort the linear term doubles Cform, at high Beaufort the 6.5-power term does the same. The choice is undocumented and reversible in one line.

What the audit did not find

  • No forecast lookahead leak. Rolling-horizon vintage selection is correct: the planner never reads a forecast issued after the decision time, and never substitutes actuals where a forecast was intended.
  • The rhumb-line geometry is right. It reproduces the benchmark paper's segment table to 0.36 NM over 3,393 NM — the loxodrome is deliberate, not accidental.
  • Direction averaging avoids the classic wrap bug. Bearings use a circular mean, so 0° and 350° do not average to 175°.
  • Wind and current conventions are correct — meteorological FROM for wind, oceanographic TOWARDS for current, each used consistently.
  • The spatial machinery is real, not decorative — the crossings genuinely place the decision lines and resolve the weather, so this is a change of where the lines go, not the introduction of a mechanism that was missing.

What changes in the graph

The rule is the same on both routes. The constraint that binds is not — so these are two decisions, and they should be made and reported separately.

The rule, and why it is exactly right

A decision point belongs where the weather partition changes. There is a reason this is the right partition rather than merely a reasonable one: within a stretch over which the weather is held constant, the fuel rate is convex in speed, so a constant speed over that stretch beats any varying profile achieving the same transit time. The optimal control is therefore piecewise constant on exactly the partition the weather is piecewise constant on — no finer partition can improve the solution, and any coarser one forfeits optimality.

Our weather partition is the set of waypoint intervals, one reading each. So H-lines belong at the waypoints, on both routes. What differs is what that costs and what it leaves unresolved.

What route 1 still cannot do

Each 25 NM segment crosses a median 8 source tiles (up to 16) and reads one of them. The graph cannot fix that — only finer collection can. Two things follow for the write-up:

  1. State the square as 25 NM and the constancy scale as ~5 NM, and say plainly that route 1's squares are five times the resolution of the data. It is a sampling-budget limitation, not a modelling error.
  2. Twelve segments run long — up to 48.99 NM, together 446 NM or 13.1% of the route — because of the collector's int(d/interval) − 1 off-by-one. Fix that for any future collection so segments are a uniform 25 NM. It cannot be fixed retrospectively.

Re-collecting route 1 finer would help only the perfect-foresight runs: rolling-horizon needs forecasts issued in real time, and those cannot be backfilled.

So this is a runtime judgement. If 3.2× proves intractable, taking every second waypoint gives 195 H-lines at 1.6× — still finer than today and still an improvement — but it reintroduces two-sample averaging and with it the aggregation questions the redesign was meant to remove. I would pay the 3.2× and keep the clean rule.

And there is a floor: do not go finer than 5 NM on route 2. Each sample sits a median 1.58 NM from the position its value actually belongs to — 32% of a 5 NM square. Below about 5 NM the position uncertainty of a sample would exceed the square it is being placed in, so 5 NM is not merely the budget that was available; it is close to the right answer on its own terms.

How to build the H-lines

The construction is shorter than the one it replaces, and it turns on a single move: make the collected waypoints the graph's polyline. Today the polyline runs through the 13 (or 11) control points and the waypoints are a separate list used only to fill cells. Promote them, and the path the graph integrates becomes the path the weather was sampled along — which removes the great-circle-versus-rhumb discrepancy rather than papering over it.

Why the boundary ambiguity disappears

Step 5 is the fix at the root. Today a node's cell is found by converting its distance to a latitude and longitude and taking floor(lat/0.5) — and on a boundary that floor is a floating-point coin-flip. In the new construction the node distances and the segment boundaries are the same numbers, so the comparison is exact. I verified that bisect_right(H, d) − 1 returns the departing segment at every H-line on both routes, with no exceptions. There is no derived coordinate to round, so there is nothing to get wrong — and position_at_d leaves the pricing path entirely, taking the segment-boundary heading bug with it.

The feasibility filter becomes a no-op

Uniform spacing makes the τ-grid check pass everywhere: 0 of 130 segments fail on route 1 and 0 of 388 on route 2. The shortest segment on route 2 is 5.00 NM, whose traverse window at 8.5–14.5 kn spans 0.345–0.588 h, or 2.4 τ steps — comfortably more than one. So the filter can stay as an assertion, and if it ever fires that is now a signal something is wrong upstream rather than routine attrition. This is what removes the ETA dependence: the filter no longer deletes lines, so the spatial grid stops being a function of the speed band.

Two consequences worth stating in the paper

The route length shifts very slightly. Measuring along the waypoint polyline gives 3393.55 NM on route 1 and 1954.03 NM on route 2. Route 1's figure sits 0.31 NM from Luo's table (3393.24) and 2.29 NM below the stored haversine axis. The published "3,393 NM" stays correct to four figures, and the three competing distance axes collapse to one.

Heading becomes per-segment rather than per-leg, and on route 2 that matters. Because the waypoints sit on a great circle, the bearing drifts within each control-point leg — a median of 0.52° and up to 1.72° on route 1, but a median of 4.32° and up to 6.30° on route 2. The new construction captures that drift instead of holding one heading per leg. It also means the paper's claim that "heading is constant in each route segment" (line 417) becomes true of the discretization segment rather than the control-point leg — a better claim, but a different one.

Six findings collapse at once

With one sample per segment, a segment's weather simply is that waypoint's reading. No cell, no aggregation. That removes:

  1. the 0.5° binning and cell_weather entirely;
  2. the empty-cell fallback — every segment carries its own sample by construction;
  3. the floor() boundary coin-flip — there are no cell boundaries to land on;
  4. the non-vector current averaging — nothing is averaged;
  5. the Beaufort quantise-then-average question — nothing is averaged;
  6. most of the ETA-dependent H-line filter, since uniform spacing makes τ-feasibility uniform.

The temporal change, and the decoupling it requires

Shrink the constancy block from 6 h toward 1 h. The hourly data is already in the file, so this costs no collection. But keep the re-plan cadence at 6 h: the forecast issue is 6-hourly, which is the right reason to re-plan on that cycle, while the forecast itself is hourly. The paper currently binds both to one number; they must be separated in the text and in run_rh.

Order of work

  1. Correctness fixes first — free, and independent of every decision above. Probe the interior of the cell being entered rather than the point on its line; return the outgoing segment's heading at a boundary; vector-average currents while cell_weather still exists.
  2. Route 1: move H-lines to the waypoints. No trade-off to weigh — 163 → 131 lines at 0.8× cost. Needs only agreement that the rule is right.
  3. Route 2: decide the 3.2×. A runtime call. Measure it before committing, and keep the 195-line fallback in reserve rather than as the plan.
  4. Then the block, per route: 3 h on route 1 is cheap; on route 2 it is not. Measure before choosing, and do not assume both routes take the same value.

One convention to state rather than inherit, on both routes: price each segment with its source waypoint's sample, matching the existing price-at-source convention, rather than a two-point mean. And note that adopting a per-route block length means the paper can no longer describe "the" discretization unit — it has to name one per route, which is more honest anyway given that the spacing already differs by 5×.

What should not change

  • The (t, d) node formulation — nodes as time–distance pairs on a fixed track.
  • The rhumb geometry — it reproduces the benchmark table to 0.36 NM.
  • The streaming solver and the node-first arc enumeration.
  • The rolling-horizon vintage selection — no lookahead leak was found.

This is a change to where the lines go, not to the method.

The honest framing

The paper justifies allowing speed to change at cell crossings on the grounds that the operative data change there. They do not. The forecast changes on a ~0.08° grid — 3–5 NM — and our knowledge of it changes only at our waypoints. Neither happens at a geographic line we drew ourselves. The decision grid and the information grid are different objects, and the fix is to make them the same object.

What can be defended, and stated exactly, is this: the spatial unit is one sampling interval, within which the forecast is constant because we hold a single reading of it. That is true on both routes by construction. On route 2, sampled at 5 NM against a 3–5 NM source tile, it is also true of the data. On route 1, sampled at 25 NM, the unit is five times the constancy scale, and that is a limitation of the sampling budget which belongs in the text.

On the spatial axis there is no version of this that reaches constancy: every practical segment crosses several source tiles, so the square can only ever be an information partition, never a physical one. The block is different. It is six times coarser than the hourly data already in the file and already paid for, and shrinking it costs nothing but compute. If one number in the discretization can actually be improved rather than merely described more honestly, it is the six hours.
