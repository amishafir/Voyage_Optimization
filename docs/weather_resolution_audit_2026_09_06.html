One granularity, one reading, one rectangle

What the forecast API actually delivers, what the model actually consumes, and what the graph's discretization unit therefore is — a rectangle in distance and time bounded by new weather readings and by changes of course.

All three fields arrive at the same resolution

Established by probing the API's resolved cell coordinates, not by reading the documentation or the paper.

Open-Meteo performs no spatial interpolation: it returns the value of whichever grid cell contains the requested point, and reports the cell it used. Sweeping coordinates and reading those back recovers each endpoint's grid exactly.

All three fields arrive at essentially the same resolution — about 0.08°, or 3–5 NM. Not 0.25° for wind against 0.08° for the marine fields.

The paper's §5.1.1 is wrong on both counts it states. Wind is attributed to GFS at 0.25°; because models is never set, the request falls back to best_match, which selects the highest-resolution applicable model — over open ocean, ECMWF IFS HRES at ~9 km. And MFWAM is given as 0.25° where the documented and probed figure is 0.08°. The premise that the components arrive at heterogeneous granularities needing reconciliation is simply not the case.

One parameter left unset that should not be

cell_selection is never sent either. The Marine API defaults to sea, which is correct for us; the Forecast API defaults to land, which seeks a grid cell on land of similar elevation. On an ocean route that is the wrong default, and near the coastal ends of both routes the wind record may be mis-sourced. The fix is one parameter.

The fuel rate consumes no weather whatsoever. FCR = 0.000706 · Vs³  (mt h⁻¹, floor 0.1) It is a pure cubic in still-water speed. Weather has exactly one point of entry into the objective: the solver fixes an arc's SOG from its distance and duration, inverts Vs = g−1(Vg; w) by binary search through steps 1–9, and prices the resulting SWS cubically.

Of the four values reaching that inversion, one is an integer 0–12, two describe the current, and one is a bearing. That is the entire weather content of the cost function — and §5.1.1 spends three bullets documenting the resolution, cadence and horizon of products supplying rather less than that.

The rectangle

Not a square, in two different senses — and its edges have two different causes.

Rectangles, not squares

Geographically: the source tile is 1/12° on both axes, but a degree of latitude is 60 NM while a degree of longitude is 60·cos(lat) NM. So the tile measures 5.00 × 4.88 NM on route 1 and 5.00 × 3.05 NM on route 2 — a rectangle that narrows with latitude, and keeps changing shape along the voyage. For wind there is no fixed rectangle at all: the reduced Gaussian grid holds cell area roughly constant rather than cell shape.

In the graph: the unit is a rectangle in the (distance, time) plane, bounded by two H-lines and two V-lines. That is the object the DP actually works on.

Its distance edges have two causes

An H-line is needed wherever the cost coefficient changes, and that has two independent causes. A new weather reading changes the resistance directly. A change of course alters the relative angle between the ship and an unchanged wind or current, so the effective weather changes even when the forecast values are identical.

So the rule is a union: H = weather points ∪ course-change points. In the current data the course-change points are already a subset of the waypoints — generate_waypoints emits every control point — so the union collapses to just the waypoints. The convexity argument needs both held constant: with weather and heading fixed across a rectangle, the fuel rate is convex in speed, so a constant speed across it beats any varying profile achieving the same transit time. That makes this partition provably the right one — no finer partition can improve the solution, and any coarser one forfeits optimality.

All the weather arrives at one granularity — about 0.08°, or 3–5 NM. That source tile is a rectangle, 5.00 NM tall and 3–5 NM wide depending on latitude, and it is the only region in which the forecast is genuinely constant. Our sampling cannot resolve it: our along-track intervals are 25 NM and 5 NM, each crossing several tiles.

So in the graph we build rectangles in distance × time. Their distance edges come from two causes — a new weather reading, and a change of course — because both change the cost coefficient. Their time edges come from the forecast block. Inside one rectangle we hold exactly one weather reading and one heading, so the model treats conditions as constant there: constant in our knowledge, not in fact. And that is the right partition regardless, because with the cost coefficient fixed across a rectangle, constant speed across it is optimal.

The one collection fix this exposes

The union is only exactly right if the sample points lie on the path the ship sails. They do not. The DP integrates rhumb legs between control points, so heading is constant within each leg — but the collector interpolated the waypoints along the great circle, so they sit off those legs by up to 1.4 NM on route 1 and 4.1 NM on route 2, and their bearings drift within a leg by a median 0.52° / 4.32°, up to 6.30° on route 2.

For any future collection the fix is one line: generate the waypoints along the rhumb line. Then all three statements hold at once — the path is a sequence of rhumb legs, heading is constant per leg, the sample points lie exactly on the path, and the control points are exactly the course-change subset of the waypoints.
