# Design — migrating the paper to the `waypoint` partition

**Date:** 2026-09-09
**Decision taken:** report the `waypoint` partition; revise the conclusions to match.
**Status:** design only, nothing applied to `paper_full_draft.tex` yet.

---

## 1. Why `waypoint`, stated once

The partition must not be chosen to protect a conclusion. `waypoint` places one block per weather
observation and assigns each block the value actually measured there. `geo` imposes a 0.5° grid and
fills each cell with an average of samples that differ — and on Malacca it builds **163 blocks out of
131 samples**, manufacturing structure that no observation supports.

`geo`'s one apparent virtue was that its block lengths come out similar across the two routes
(20.8 vs 16.2 nm), making the cross-route comparison look controlled. That evenness is an artifact of
the imposed grid, not a property of the data. `waypoint`'s uneven blocks (27.2 vs 5.0 nm) are the
honest picture: Route 1 was sampled 5× coarser than Route 2.

So `geo` did not solve the sampling imbalance, it concealed it. That settles the choice.

## 3. The claim that dies

Six places in the paper assert the gap is **larger on the harsher Atlantic route** and attribute that
to convexity. Under `waypoint` the ordering inverts, so all six become false:

| Line | Claim |
|---|---|
| 65–66 | abstract: "with the saving growing with weather variability" |
| 126–127 | intro: "the advantage grew with weather variability, larger on the rougher Atlantic route … exactly the signature the convexity argument predicts" |
| 988–991 | §7.1: "larger on the shorter, harsher Atlantic route (2.60 %) than on Malacca (1.80 %)" |
| 1110–1112 | §8.1: "the gap scaled with weather variability … as expected for a penalty driven by within-block weather spread" |
| 1149–1150 | §8.4: "with the gap growing with weather variability exactly as the convexity argument predicts" |
| 1186 | §9: "proportionally larger on the shorter, harsher voyage" |

### Why losing it is informative rather than damaging

Convexity does not predict that the gap grows with *weather variability*. It predicts the gap grows
with **within-block weather spread**, and that quantity is

```
within-block spread  ≈  block length  ×  local weather gradient
```

Our two routes differ in **both factors, in opposite directions**: Malacca has long blocks and a mild
gradient; Atlantic has short blocks and a steep one. Two variables, two observations — the
cross-route ordering was never identified:

| Partition | block-length ratio (M : A) | ordering |
|---|---|---|
| `geo` | 1.29× | Atlantic > Malacca |
| `waypoint` | 5.39× | Malacca > Atlantic |

The ordering flips with the partition, so it is a property of the discretization as much as of the
weather. §4 goes further and shows that **neither** factor can be credited: the causal reading of the
flip runs backwards.

So the old claim was not merely wrong under `waypoint` — it was **unidentified under either
partition**, and `geo` made it look supported. This is a correction to the paper's evidence, not to
its theory: convexity still predicts a penalty in within-block spread, and SR still beats Luo on
every voyage. What we never had was the measurement to say how that penalty scales.

## 4. What replaces it — nothing yet, and that is the point

The paper's thesis is already the right one: §8.2 is titled *"Decision granularity, not data, is the
limiting factor."* The fix is to support it with a controlled manipulation instead of a confounded
route contrast.

### First, a structural fact that constrains every interpretation

**Luo's block is the 6 h forecast cycle, not the spatial partition.** It solves 47 blocks on Malacca
(280 h ÷ 47 = 5.96 h) and 28 on Atlantic (168 ÷ 28 = 6.00 h), and those counts are **identical under
both partitions**. So the partition changes only (a) SR's leg resolution and (b) the weather values
both planners see. Since Luo's fuel barely moves, `gap ≈ −SR`.

### The geo↔waypoint contrast cannot carry the granularity claim — it points the wrong way

| Route | blocks geo → waypoint | SR decisions | SR fuel | gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Malacca | 20.82 → 27.15 nm (**longer**) | **fewer** | 347.07 → 345.46 (**better**) | −1.77 → **−2.43 %** |
| Atlantic | 16.15 → 5.04 nm (**shorter**) | **more** | 196.33 → 198.78 (**worse**) | −2.54 → **−1.40 %** |

Malacca gets *fewer* decision points and SR improves; Atlantic gets *more* and SR degrades. Expressed
per Luo block, Atlantic `waypoint` gives SR **10.7–15.5 legs per 6 h block** — by far the most freedom
of any configuration — and yields the **smallest** gap of the four.

So the gap is monotone in block length within each route only as a two-point correlation; the causal
reading is inverted. The driver of the SR change is the **weather-assignment switch** (cell average →
point value), not granularity. Its mechanism is **not established** — note it does not even act in a
consistent direction across routes (Malacca −1.61 mt, Atlantic +2.45 mt).

**Conclusion: the two partitions provide no evidence about granularity scaling at all.** This is
stronger than "confounded" — the contrast must not be used for the claim in either direction.

### The experiment that makes it rigorous

**A stride sweep on the `waypoint` partition.** Keep every *N*-th usable sample point, which varies
block length while holding the weather-assignment rule, the route, the voyages and the code fixed.
Nothing is interpolated — every block still carries an observed value.

| Route | stride | blocks | block length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Atlantic | 1 | 388 | 5.0 nm |
| Atlantic | 2 | ~194 | 10.1 nm |
| Atlantic | 5 | ~78 | 25.1 nm |
| Atlantic | 10 | ~39 | 50.1 nm |
| Malacca | 1 | 125 | 27.2 nm |
| Malacca | 2 | ~62 | 54.3 nm |

This yields a **dose–response curve for decision granularity** — the paper's central thesis measured
rather than asserted. Because the weather-assignment rule is held fixed, it is the only clean test
available: it isolates the one variable the geo↔waypoint contrast hopelessly entangles. It also
answers the cross-route question as a by-product: Atlantic at stride 5 (25.1 nm) is directly
comparable to Malacca at stride 1 (27.2 nm), so the route comparison finally holds block length fixed.

Interpret it **relative to Luo's fixed 6 h block** (54–78 nm of travel), not in absolute nm. SR's
advantage should rise as its legs subdivide that block, then saturate once further subdivision buys
no new information. Atlantic stride 1 already sits at 10.7–15.5 legs per block, plausibly past
saturation — which would explain why the finest configuration shows the smallest gap and would make
saturation the headline finding rather than a caveat.

The sweep is therefore **required, not optional**: without it the paper has no evidence for how the
gap scales with anything.

**Implementation:** a `--sample_stride N` flag in the `waypoint` branch of `frame.cpp` and `frame.py`,
subject to the usual cross-engine bit-exactness check, plus a golden-matrix entry. Stride 1 must
reproduce today's numbers exactly — that is the regression test.

**Cost:** ~30 lines per engine; Atlantic 26 voyages × 4 strides ≈ 90 min; Malacca 15 × 2 ≈ 25 min.

with weather variability *or* with block length. Until the stride sweep lands, every scaling claim

and gives up its magnitude claim.

- **SR beats Luo on every voyage.** 41/41 under `waypoint`, and 41/41 under `geo` too. The

## 6. The rolling-horizon coupling — the biggest open item

§7.2 (`tab:rh`, `tab:rh-r1`, `tab:rh-r2`) comes from `runs/2026_06_15_rh_cpp_chain/`, which has **no
partition column** — it predates the partition work and is therefore `geo`, on the 19-voyage subset.

Two consequences:

1. **Mixed partitions.** Moving §7.1 to `waypoint` while §7.2 stays `geo` means the paper's two
   results sections discretize space differently. §8's cross-references between them ("the advantage
   persisted under realistic operation") would compare across a changed graph.
2. **Same reproducibility defect.** That June-15 run is exactly the one whose Malacca oracle numbers
   no longer reproduce (see the Sep-14 prep §5J). Its Naive figures shifted by ~1 mt, and `tab:rh`
   reports percentages *relative to Naive*, so the published RH percentages are stale as well.

So §7.2 must be re-run regardless of the partition decision. Doing it on `waypoint` at 41 voyages
resolves both problems at once.

**Blocker:** `pipeline/dp_cpp/run_rh_chain.py` still carries hardcoded 19-voyage departure lists
(four literal lists across two engines). It needs the same `chain_sh_bases()` treatment already
applied to `run_pf_chain.py`.

**Also blocked:** the oracle reference constants are `geo`-only, which makes the RH `≥ oracle` gate
meaningless on `waypoint`. That gate has to be re-derived from the new PF run before RH results can
be trusted.

## 7. The Route 1 sampling limitation — state it, do not hide it

Route 1 samples every 25.0 nm; Route 2 every 5.0 nm. The 0.08° grid could have supplied a value every
~4.8 nm, so **Route 1's collection took one point in five**.

Under `waypoint` this is visible rather than concealed, and it must be stated plainly: the reported
Malacca blocks are 27 nm not because the weather is known to be uniform over 27 nm, but because that
is how often it was sampled. The paper's §3 claim of 0.08° discretization is satisfied on Atlantic and
**not** on Malacca.

Two ways to discharge it:

- **Cheap and honest:** state it as a data limitation, and let the stride sweep (§4) show what block
  length costs. This is sufficient for the current paper.
- **Thorough:** re-collect Route 1 at 5 nm. A data run, not a model change, but it invalidates nothing
  — it would simply add a finer point to the dose–response curve.

Recommend the first for this paper, the second as future work.

| 6 | Write the granularity result into §7.1/§8.1 on the sweep's evidence | ~1 h | 5 |

| 8 | Re-run RH, 41 voyages, `waypoint`; regenerate §7.2 and §8 cross-refs | hours | 7 |

Steps 1–2 are self-contained, fix a live defect (non-reproducible numbers, plus an abstract that
contradicts its own results section) and can land immediately. **Step 3 is the cheapest high-value
item in the list** — half an hour to learn whether granularity contributes anything to the
geo↔waypoint difference. Steps 4–6 are what let the paper say anything at all about scaling. Steps
7–8 are required for internal consistency between §7.1 and §7.2 but do not block the others.

Note the reordering relative to a first reading: the stride sweep is no longer an optional
strengthening move that comes last. Because §5 deletes the magnitude claim outright, steps 4–6 are
what restore the paper's ability to explain *why* the gap is the size it is.

## 9. Risks and open questions

- **The paper gets weaker before it gets stronger.** Between step 2 and step 7 the paper has retired
  its weather-variability claim without yet having the dose–response result. If the deadline does not
  allow steps 6–7, the honest interim position is: report `waypoint`, state that the cross-route
  ordering is not attributable to weather because block length differs 5.4×, and leave the granularity
  scaling as a stated open question. That is defensible; the old claim was not.
- **The stride sweep could fail to show monotonicity.** If the gap does not rise with block length,
  the convexity mechanism is not the dominant driver and §8.1 needs deeper revision. Worth knowing
  either way, and better found by us than by a referee.
- **Is `Luo` a fair comparator at 5 nm blocks? — resolved, yes.** Luo's block is the 6 h forecast
  cycle (47 / 28 blocks, unchanged across partitions), not the spatial partition, so it is not
  handicapped by a fine partition. Verified directly.
- **Why does SR degrade on Atlantic under `waypoint`? — the central unresolved question.** SR gains
  3× the decision points and burns 2.45 mt *more*. Candidate explanations: (i) point-sampled weather
  is genuinely harsher than the cell average it replaces, so the voyage really does cost more and the
  `geo` figure was optimistic; (ii) at 10.7–15.5 legs per Luo block, SR is past the point where extra
  legs add exploitable information, so the resolution gain is nil while the weather change bites.
  These have different consequences: under (i) the `waypoint` numbers are simply the correct, harsher
  truth; under (ii) there is a genuine saturation result worth reporting. **Distinguishing them is the
  first thing the stride sweep should be read for**, and it should be settled before §7.1's prose is
  finalised.
- **A cheap decisive probe for (i):** re-run `geo` with point-sampled weather at the cell's source
  node instead of the cell average. If SR's fuel then matches the `waypoint` figure, the weather
  assignment is the whole story and granularity contributes nothing to the geo↔waypoint difference.
- Mean-of-percentages vs fuel-weighted aggregation differ slightly (pooled `waypoint`: +1.76 % vs
  +1.92 %). The paper should say which it reports; `make_pf_tables.py` currently uses ratio-of-means,
  matching the published convention.
